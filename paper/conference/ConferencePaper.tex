\begin{abstract}
Fine-tuned large language models (LLMs) are now a common choice for rating product reviews, but they are usually deployed as a single model that reads a review once, outputs a star rating and, at best, a free-text justification that nothing checks. We take a recently published method, LLaMA-3-8B fine-tuned with LoRA for five-star review rating, as our reference paper, reproduce it on a single, leakage-controlled dataset (Amazon Reviews 2023, Electronics) and explore where it holds and where it does not. The reproduction exposes three problems: the reported accuracy depends strongly on the evaluation set-up, the paper's data-cleaning step removes most negative reviews, and the model's justifications are a fixed template. We then build an agentic system around these findings. Three independent agents (a probe on the frozen LLaMA-3-8B hidden state, a TF-IDF evidence model and a fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3 encoder) are combined by a learned Reflector; an Explainer writes an explanation from evidence phrases and a Critic checks it and forces rewrites; a Hand-off agent answers only when the system is confident. On a balanced test set of 1\,000 reviews and a natural test set of 2\,000 reviews, the system reaches 67.5\% and 78.0\% exact star accuracy, against 54.0\% and 53.4\% for the reproduced method (29\% and 53\% fewer errors, significant after Holm correction). The Critic loop raises the share of faithful explanations from 62--64\% to 90.5--91.6\% without breaking any explanation that was already faithful, and a deletion test shows that the cited phrases are the ones the decision depends on. The Hand-off agent answers 56\% of natural reviews automatically with 92.8\% accuracy. Every design choice was fixed on development data with a rule written before the run; failed ideas, including three attempts to let an LLM decide the star, are reported.
\keywords{Sentiment analysis \and LLaMA-3 \and Multi-agent systems \and Self-correction \and Faithful explanations \and Selective prediction \and Reproducibility}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Online retailers receive far more reviews than any team can read, and star ratings alone hide why a customer was satisfied or not. Automated sentiment analysis therefore remains a core e-commerce task \cite{pang2008}. Pre-trained transformers \cite{devlin2019bert,he2021debertav3} and, more recently, instruction-tuned LLMs \cite{grattafiori2024llama3,zhang2023sentiment} have raised the accuracy of review classification, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning such as LoRA \cite{hu2022lora} and QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora} makes an 8-billion-parameter model trainable on a single GPU.

Wang \cite{wang2025} follows this route: LLaMA-3-8B is fine-tuned with LoRA on Amazon product reviews, after a data pipeline of TextBlob-based quality control, stratified sampling, diversity sampling and neutral oversampling, and is prompted with a rating rubric, one worked example and a step-by-step cue. The paper reports 92.6\% accuracy. We chose this paper as our reference for three reasons: it is recent, it uses an open model that can be run locally, and it describes its pipeline in enough detail to be rebuilt.

A system of this kind has three structural limits. First, it is a \emph{single pass}: one model reads the review once, and nothing disagrees with it or catches a mistake. Second, its \emph{justification is not checked}: the model writes a sentence about the rating, but nothing verifies that the sentence is supported by the review. Third, its reported accuracy is a \emph{single number on a single set-up}, which says little about how the method behaves under a different test distribution or a different prompt.

This paper is organised in two phases. In Phase~1 we reproduce the reference method on one dataset with strict leakage controls and explore it: which steps help, which do not, and what the reported number depends on. In Phase~2 we design an agentic system that answers each problem found in Phase~1. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A leakage-controlled reproduction and exploration of the reference method on Amazon Reviews 2023 Electronics, including a one-shot example leak worth about 8 points, a data-cleaning step that deletes most negative reviews, and a study showing that the model's justifications are a template (Section~\ref{sec:phase1}).
\item A three-agent rating core (a probe on the frozen LLaMA-3-8B hidden state, a TF-IDF evidence model, and a fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3 encoder) combined by a learned Reflector, which improves exact accuracy by 13.5 and 24.6 points over the reproduced method (Sections~\ref{sec:phase2} and \ref{sec:results}).
\item An agentic layer around this core: an Explainer and a Critic that make 90.5--91.6\% of explanations faithful with no explanation broken, a deletion test that checks the cited evidence, and a Hand-off agent that answers automatically only when the system is confident (Section~\ref{sec:agentic}).
\item A complete account of the selection process: every candidate was accepted or rejected by a rule declared before the run and checked on development data only, and the failed candidates are reported (Section~\ref{sec:failed}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{Sentiment analysis and review rating.} Early work framed review sentiment as polarity or rating prediction over bag-of-words features \cite{pang2008}; term weighting such as TF-IDF \cite{salton1988} with a linear classifier remains a strong baseline. Sentiment benchmarks such as SST \cite{socher2013} and IMDB \cite{maas2011} drove the move to neural models, and fine-tuned encoders such as BERT \cite{devlin2019bert}, DistilBERT \cite{sanh2019distilbert} and DeBERTa \cite{he2021deberta,he2021debertav3} became the default. Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2023sentiment} find that LLMs are competitive on simple polarity tasks but lag fine-tuned models on fine-grained rating, which matches what we observe for zero-shot LLaMA-3. Large Amazon review corpora \cite{ni2019,hou2024} provide the data for this task; we use the 2023 release \cite{hou2024}.

\textbf{Fine-tuning and probing LLMs.} LoRA \cite{hu2022lora} trains low-rank update matrices on a frozen model, and QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora} does so on a 4-bit quantised model. An alternative that needs no fine-tuning is a linear probe on a model's hidden states \cite{alain2016probes}; probes must be read with care \cite{hewitt2019control}, but they are a cheap way to extract what a frozen model already encodes. We use both: LoRA in Phase~1, as the reference paper does, and a probe in Phase~2.

\textbf{Combining models.} Stacked generalisation \cite{wolpert1992} trains a second-level model on the outputs of several first-level models; it works best when the first-level models make different errors. Our Reflector is a stacker in this sense. We report calibration with the expected calibration error \cite{guo2017calibration}.

\textbf{Self-correction, reflection and multi-agent LLM systems.} Chain-of-thought prompting \cite{wei2022cot,kojima2022zeroshot} and self-consistency \cite{wang2023selfconsistency} improve LLM reasoning; Self-Refine \cite{madaan2023selfrefine} and Reflexion \cite{shinn2023reflexion} let a model critique and revise its own output, Self-RAG \cite{asai2023selfrag} adds retrieval and self-critique, ReAct \cite{yao2023react} interleaves reasoning and tool use, and multi-agent frameworks \cite{du2023debate,wu2023autogen} let several LLM agents discuss or debate. Huang et al.\ \cite{huang2024cannot} show, however, that LLMs often cannot correct their own answers without external feedback, and LLM judges have known biases \cite{zheng2023judge}. Our findings agree: an LLM asked to decide or revise the star failed three times, so in our system the LLM explains and checks, while the star is decided by the Reflector, and every check uses external signals (the review text, the evidence model and the deletion test).

\textbf{Faithful explanations.} An explanation is faithful if it reflects the reasons the model actually used \cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}; LLM explanations are often plausible but unfaithful \cite{turpin2023unfaithful}. ERASER \cite{deyoung2020eraser} measures faithfulness by deleting the cited tokens (comprehensiveness) or keeping only them (sufficiency); post-hoc methods such as LIME \cite{ribeiro2016lime} attribute predictions to input features. We apply the ERASER deletion test to the phrases our explanations cite.

\textbf{Abstaining and uncertainty.} A classifier can abstain when unsure; the error--reject trade-off goes back to Chow \cite{chow1970}, and selective classification for neural networks is studied by Geifman and El-Yaniv \cite{geifman2017selective}. Conformal prediction \cite{angelopoulos2021conformal} turns scores into prediction sets with a coverage guarantee. LLMs can partly estimate their own uncertainty \cite{kadavath2022know}. Our Hand-off agent combines both ideas.

\textbf{Evaluation and leakage.} Data leakage inflates many published machine-learning results \cite{kapoor2023leakage}; behavioural tests such as CheckList \cite{ribeiro2020checklist} probe robustness beyond accuracy. We use frozen, disjoint splits, declare every test before running it, and report robustness under controlled text changes.

\textbf{Research gap.} The reference paper reports a single accuracy on its own set-up and does not check its justifications. Self-correction work mostly targets reasoning benchmarks and often lets the LLM grade itself. We are not aware of work that reproduces a fine-tuned LLM review-rating method under leakage control and then measures, on the same frozen test sets, what independent agents, a learned combiner, a Critic loop on explanations and an abstaining hand-off each add.

\section{Data and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:data}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

All main results use one dataset, the Electronics category of Amazon Reviews 2023 \cite{hou2024}. Each review has a title, a text and a star rating from 1 to 5; we use title and text only. (Product images were tested in an earlier design and excluded, Section~\ref{sec:failed}.) The splits were drawn once with a fixed seed and frozen before any model was trained. They are disjoint by review identifier and by exact text, so no review, and no duplicate of a review, appears in two splits (Table~\ref{tbl:splits}).

\textbf{Two test sets.} The balanced test set (eval) asks whether the system can tell every star apart; a model that always predicts the most common star scores 20\%. The natural test set (natural\_test) asks how it performs on the real mix, where always predicting 5 stars already scores about 65\%. We report both, because a model can look good on one and poor on the other.

\textbf{Metrics.} The main metric is exact accuracy over the five stars. We also report macro-F1, the share of predictions within one star, mean absolute error (MAE) and quadratic weighted kappa \cite{cohen1968kappa}, which credit near misses. Confidence intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals over 3\,000 resamples \cite{efron1979}. Two systems are compared on the same reviews with the exact McNemar test \cite{mcnemar1947}, and $p$-values are adjusted for multiple comparisons with the Holm procedure \cite{holm1979}.

\textbf{Gates.} Every candidate change was declared in a dated log before it ran, together with a pass rule (a ``gate''), for example ``accept if dev2 accuracy improves by at least 1.0 point and the paired confidence interval excludes zero''. Gates are checked on dev, dev2 and natural\_dev only. A candidate that fails is never scored on eval or natural\_test, so the test sets are read once, by the final system and its reported comparisons.

\section{Phase 1: Reproducing and Exploring the Reference Paper}
\label{sec:phase1}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{The reference method}

Wang \cite{wang2025} rates a review from 1 to 5 stars with LLaMA-3-8B fine-tuned by LoRA. The pipeline has four data steps: (1) TextBlob data-quality control removes reviews whose polarity contradicts the star; (2) stratified sampling takes the same number of reviews per star; (3) a greedy diversity search (VGST) prefers reviews with unseen tokens; (4) neutral (3-star) reviews are oversampled. The LoRA update $h = Wx + BAx$ with $B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$, $r \ll d$ is trained with rank $r=4$, $\alpha=64$, learning rate $5\cdot10^{-5}$ and 3 epochs. The prompt contains a rating rubric, one worked example (one-shot) and a step-by-step cue. The paper reports 92.6\% accuracy and 93.3\% F1.

\subsection{Our reproduction}

We trained the method on 2\,000 Electronics reviews (400 per star) with the paper's hyperparameters, using QLoRA on the 4-bit LLaMA-3-8B, on an Apple M1 laptop with 16\,GB of memory. Inference reads the model's probabilities for the digits 1--5, so every review gets a full probability vector as well as a star. We call this model ``Phase~1'' throughout.

On the paper's own data source (a Kaggle collection of Amazon reviews, with the paper's protocol, 4\,000 natural reviews and no leak) the reproduction reaches 69.1\% exact accuracy, 85.9\% polarity accuracy and 93.5\% within one star. On Electronics it reaches 54.0\% exact on the balanced test set and 53.4\% on the natural test set (Table~\ref{tbl:main}). It gets the direction right (91.2\% and 92.9\% correct on positive versus negative, 3-star reviews excluded; 93.7\% and 94.3\% within one star) but often confuses neighbouring stars.

\subsection{What the exploration found}

\textbf{The score depends on the set-up.} In our first reproduction the one-shot example was drawn from reviews whose star differed from the star of the review being rated. The example therefore told the model one answer that was wrong. A controlled comparison showed that this choice raised accuracy by about 8 points ($-7.7$ points when removed, 95\% CI $[-13.3, -2.0]$), while a zero-shot prompt cost only $-1.3$ points (not significant). All results in this paper use the zero-shot prompt. A second effect is the test distribution: on a natural test set where 63.8\% of reviews are 5-star, always answering 5 stars already scores 63.8\%. The 92.6\% in the reference paper was measured with a different protocol (five-fold cross-validation on its own data), so it is not directly comparable to our numbers, and we do not claim that it is wrong.

\textbf{The data cleaning backfires on negative reviews.} TextBlob assigns a positive polarity to many negative reviews, because complaints often contain words such as ``great'' or ``love''. On Electronics, TextBlob filtering deleted 52\% of 1-star and 67\% of 2-star reviews, and models trained after filtering were 2.5--3.5 points worse. The diversity search added +0.2 points (noise) and biased the sample towards long reviews (mean 119 words against 61). On dev, a model trained on raw balanced data beat the paper's pipeline by 3.0 points (55.2\% against 52.2\%), so the final Phase~1 model uses raw balanced data.

\textbf{The justifications are a template.} We inspected 150 generated justifications. None was unparseable, but 80 (53\%) gave a star different from the true one, 20 were off by two stars or more, and the text was one fixed rubric sentence per digit, repeated with no reference to the review. The probabilities carry the useful signal; the generated text does not explain the decision.

\textbf{Fine-tuning helps, but less than a second signal.} The frozen base model with the same zero-shot prompt reaches only 34.3\% (eval) and 16.8\% (natural\_test), so LoRA adds 19.7 and 36.6 points. A DistilBERT encoder \cite{sanh2019distilbert} trained on the same 2\,000 reviews reaches 52.8\%, level with Phase~1 (difference not significant). Training LoRA on 10\,000 instead of 2\,000 reviews did not pass its gate (dev 56.4\% against a required 58\%). By contrast, simply stacking the Phase~1 probabilities with a TF-IDF model trained on 100\,000 reviews raised accuracy to 63.9\% and 73.4\%. This observation shaped Phase~2.

\textbf{Summary of shortcomings.} The reference method (i) reads the review once with nothing to catch mistakes, (ii) has a reported score that depends on the set-up, (iii) removes most negative reviews in its cleaning step, and (iv) produces justifications that are not grounded in the review. Phase~2 addresses each point (Table~\ref{tbl:solves}).

\section{Phase 2: An Agentic System Built on the Findings}
\label{sec:phase2}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the system. A review passes through a \emph{decision core} of three agents and a Reflector, which produce the star and its probability. An \emph{agentic layer} then decides whether to answer automatically, writes an explanation from evidence, and checks it. The system is implemented as a LangGraph state graph; each agent reads and writes a shared state record.

\subsection{The decision core}

\textbf{LLaMA probe.} Instead of fine-tuning LLaMA, we run the frozen 4-bit base LLaMA-3-8B on the zero-shot prompt and read the hidden state of the last prompt token at layer 20 (of 32), a 4\,096-dimensional vector. A logistic regression (regularisation $C=0.001$) fit on 40\,000 training reviews maps this vector to five star probabilities. The probe uses what the language model already encodes about the review, without training the model itself. A probe fit on 120\,000 reviews was no better (dev2 $+0.3$ points, not significant), so 40\,000 is kept.

\textbf{Evidence model.} A logistic regression over TF-IDF unigrams and bigrams of the title and text \cite{salton1988}, trained on about 497\,000 balanced reviews. It is fast, and its weights tell which phrases in a review push towards or away from each star. These phrases form the \emph{evidence pack} used by the Explainer.

\textbf{DeBERTa agent.} DeBERTa-v3-base \cite{he2021debertav3} fine-tuned for five-way rating on 100\,000 balanced training reviews (title and text, 256 tokens, 2 epochs). It reads word order and context, which the TF-IDF model cannot.

\textbf{Reflector.} A multinomial logistic regression (regularisation $C=1$) on the concatenated log-probabilities of the three agents (15 features), fit on the 2\,000 dev2 reviews \cite{wolpert1992}. It learns how much to trust each agent for each star; for example, it learns that the Evidence model is reliable on short reviews and the probe on long ones (Section~\ref{sec:why}). Its output is the final star and a probability for each star.

\textbf{Why these agents.} The three agents look at a review in different ways: the probe through the internal representation of a large generative model, the Evidence model through individual phrases, and DeBERTa through a fine-tuned encoder. A stacker gains most when its inputs make different errors \cite{wolpert1992}. The set was chosen in two gated steps: the probe and Evidence model were selected on dev2 among five candidate agent sets (gate: $+1.0$ point and a paired CI above zero; result $+3.9$, CI $[+2.4, +5.8]$), and DeBERTa was added later after passing its own gate (dev2 $+2.0$, CI $[+0.6, +3.35]$).

\subsection{The agentic layer}

\textbf{Hand-off agent.} Exact rating is hard because neighbouring stars are often ambiguous even to people. Instead of always answering, the Hand-off agent compares the Reflector's top probability with a threshold. Above it, the star is released automatically. Below it, the system either returns a short star range with 90\% coverage (split conformal prediction \cite{angelopoulos2021conformal}, calibrated on dev2 for eval and on natural\_dev for natural\_test), when that range is at most two stars wide, or hands the review to a person. The threshold was set on dev2 so that the system answers about 40\% of balanced reviews, before the test sets were read.

\textbf{Explainer.} Llama-3-8B-Instruct receives the review, the final star and the evidence pack (the review's phrases that most support the star and those that most argue against it) and writes a short explanation. It is instructed to use only the review and the evidence.

\textbf{Critic.} The Critic runs five checks on each explanation: (1) the stated star matches the system's star; (2) every quoted phrase occurs in the review or the evidence pack; (3) the explanation quotes at least two of the top evidence phrases; (4) if the agents disagree, the explanation says so; (5) an LLM judge (the same Instruct model) finds no claim that the review does not support. Checks 1--4 are deterministic. If any check fails, the Critic returns specific feedback (for example ``Remove the unsupported claim: \ldots'' or ``Quote at least 2 of these phrases: \ldots'') and the Explainer rewrites, at most four times. Explanations that still fail are labelled as such rather than hidden.

\textbf{Design rule: LLMs explain, they do not decide.} Three attempts to let an LLM decide or revise the star failed their gates (Section~\ref{sec:failed}). In the final system, the star comes only from the decision core, and the LLM agents work on the explanation, where their output can be checked against the review.

\textbf{Stability.} Results from one training run can be lucky. Over five seeds (bootstrapping the probe's fit set and dev2), the two-agent stack scores $65.8 \pm 0.6$ (eval) and $75.4 \pm 0.8$ (natural\_test), and its gain over each single agent is positive in 5 of 5 seeds on both test sets. With three DeBERTa seeds, the gain from adding DeBERTa is $+2.2 \pm 0.2$ points on natural\_test (stable) and $+0.1 \pm 0.3$ on eval (no gain), which confirms the pattern above.

\textbf{Where the system errs.} Of its 325 errors on eval, 91.7\% are one star off; on natural\_test 88.2\% of 440 errors are. The hardest classes are 2 and 3 stars (recall .51--.58); 1 and 5 stars are easiest (.72--.88). Figure~\ref{fig:cm} shows the confusion matrix: mistakes sit next to the diagonal. Phase~2 finds more reviews of every star than Phase~1 on eval except 4 stars (.630 against .635); the largest difference is on 5-star reviews (.855 against .490 on natural\_test), the most common class.

On the M1 laptop, the probe costs about 0.67 s per review (one forward pass to layer 20), DeBERTa 86 ms, and the Evidence model and Reflector well under 0.1 ms each.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Why Several Agents Beat One}
\label{sec:why}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% SRC: analysis_C.out, hard_cases.out

The gain comes from the agents being strong on different reviews (Figure~\ref{fig:complement}). On the shortest quarter of eval reviews, the Evidence model is right 70.0\% of the time and the probe only 54.6\%: a short review is mostly a few telling words. On the longest quarter, the order reverses (probe 64.3\%, Evidence 55.8\%): long reviews mix praise and complaint, and the language model's representation handles the context better. The full system is best or near-best in every length band (71.2\% and 66.3\%).

When the probe and the Evidence model disagree on the star (36\% of eval reviews, 28\% of natural ones), each alone is right only 36--50\% of the time, but the full system picks the right star 57.8\% (eval) and 62.0\% (natural\_test) of the time. The Reflector has learned which agent to trust in which situation, which is the purpose of a learned combiner.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{\efigdir agents_complement.png}
\caption{Left: exact accuracy by review length (quartiles) for the Evidence model, the probe and the full system. Right: accuracy on reviews where the probe and the Evidence model disagree.}
\label{fig:complement}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Hard cases.} We defined three hard subsets before reading the results, using TextBlob polarity and contrast words. On reviews that \emph{sound negative but are rated 4--5 stars}, the full system beats Phase~1 by 32.5 points on eval (67.5\% against 35.0\%, $n=40$, $p=.0023$) and by 35.2 points on natural\_test (78.2\% against 43.0\%, $n=193$, $p=5.4\cdot10^{-16}$). On reviews that \emph{sound positive but are rated 1--2 stars}, the gain is about 10 points but not significant ($n=72$ and 68). On mixed reviews with a contrast word, the gain is 10.0 points on eval ($p=.0032$) and 3.2 on natural\_test (not significant). Explicit sarcasm cue words were too rare to measure (6 and 1 reviews), so we make no claim about sarcasm.

\label{sec:agentic}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Explanations and the Critic loop}

We ran the Explainer and Critic on all 3\,000 test reviews. An explanation counts as \emph{faithful} when it passes all five checks. Table~\ref{tbl:faith} shows the effect of the loop.

\textbf{The loop works and is safe.} About 37\% of first drafts fail at least one check. After at most four rewrites, 90.5\% (eval) and 91.6\% (natural\_test) of explanations are faithful. The loop never broke an explanation that was already faithful (0 of 3\,000), because a rewrite is only requested when a check fails. The cost is modest: 2.9--3.0 LLM calls per review on average, about 11 s for the Explainer and 2 s for the Critic per review on the M1. Allowing four rewrites instead of two passed its own gate and fixed 17 + 46 more explanations.

\textbf{What the Critic catches.} On the first drafts, the most frequent failures are quotes that do not occur in the review or the evidence pack (194 on eval, 367 on natural\_test), missing top evidence phrases (183 and 289), and claims the judge finds unsupported (71 and 180). A wrong star is rare (7 and 9) and the agent-disagreement check never fired.

\textbf{A worked example.} One balanced-eval review used in the live demonstration is a 2-star review of a backpack: the reviewer likes the many pockets and the waterproof surface, but says the quality is questionable because a zipper broke within four months, and calls the purchase disappointing. The system rates it 2 stars. The evidence pack lists ``disappointing'', ``Poor'' and ``lacks'' as supporting the star and ``great'' and ``easy'' as arguing against it. The first draft attributes the words ``Poor quality'' to the reviewer, a phrase that does not occur in the review; the Critic replies ``Remove the unsupported claim''. The second draft removes it but quotes only one of the top evidence phrases; the Critic asks it to quote at least two. The third draft quotes ``questionable'', ``disappointing'', ``lacks space'' and ``Poor'' from the review and passes all five checks. This is what the move from 62\% to 91\% means for a single review.

\textbf{The cited phrases carry the decision.} A faithful explanation should cite what the decision actually depends on \cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}. Following ERASER \cite{deyoung2020eraser}, we removed the cited phrases from each review and re-ran the decision core, and compared this with removing the same number of random words (Figure~\ref{fig:deletion}). Removing the cited phrases lowers the system's probability of its star about twice as much as removing random words (.156 against .077 on eval, .209 against .119 on natural\_test), and keeping only the cited phrases preserves much more of it (.521 against .301; .616 against .349). All differences are significant (Wilcoxon \cite{wilcoxon1945} $p < 10^{-54}$).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{\efigdir deletion_test.png}
\caption{Deletion test. Left: drop in the probability of the system's star when the cited phrases (red) or the same number of random words (grey) are removed; higher means the cited words matter more. Right: probability with only those words kept.}
\label{fig:deletion}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Compared with Phase~1.} On the same 150 balanced reviews, judged by the same model, Phase~1's justification quotes the review in 0\% of cases and Phase~2's in 75.3\% ($p = 1.9\cdot10^{-34}$). Phase~1 produced only 5 distinct sentences (3.3\% distinct outputs) and repeated its template in 54.7\% of outputs; every Phase~2 explanation was distinct. The judge found no unsupported claim in 93.3\% of Phase~2 explanations, and in the 22 reviews where only one system passed, Phase~2 passed 17 times and Phase~1 5 times ($p=.017$).

\textbf{Limits.} About 9\% of final explanations still fail a check, mostly the LLM judge (check 5). The judge is the same model that writes the explanation, so it is not an independent check; the deterministic checks, the deletion test and the comparison with Phase~1 are. A second judge (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) was tried and failed its gate: it rejected every explanation, whatever its content.

\subsection{The Hand-off agent}

Figure~\ref{fig:handoff} shows the trade-off between how many reviews the system answers automatically (coverage) and how often it is right on those. At the threshold fixed on dev2, the system answers 56.1\% of natural reviews automatically and is right on 92.8\% of them (95\% CI $[91.3, 94.3]$); on balanced eval it answers 41.1\% and is right on 86.9\%. Phase~1 with the same rule reaches only 67.9\% and 66.9\%. The reviews passed on are genuinely hard: the system's own star is right on only 54--59\% of them.

\subsection{The Router: useful with two agents, off with three}
\label{sec:router}

The LLaMA probe is the slowest agent. The Router skips it when the Evidence model is already confident, and otherwise calls it and lets the Reflector revise the draft star (``escalation''). For the two-agent stack, the Router skipped the probe on 39\% (eval) and 48\% (natural\_test) of reviews with no significant loss of accuracy (67.4\% against 67.5\%; 75.5\% against 75.7\%), and escalation fixed more stars than it broke (92 against 50 on eval, $p=5.3\cdot10^{-4}$; 152 against 78 on natural\_test, $p=1.2\cdot10^{-6}$). When DeBERTa was added, the same rule was re-checked on dev data and failed: on natural\_dev the routed system was 0.6 points below the full one, and escalation fixed 50 and broke 37 stars ($p=.20$). As declared beforehand, the Router is therefore switched off and every review uses all three agents.

\textbf{Text changes.} In the spirit of behavioural testing \cite{ribeiro2020checklist}, we changed 300 eval reviews (60 per star) in three controlled ways and counted how often the star changed (Table~\ref{tbl:robust}). Lowercasing and removing punctuation changes the Phase~2 star for 9.3\% of reviews, against 29.7\% for Phase~1. Typos barely matter for either. Both the Evidence model and DeBERTa read the title, so the three-agent system depends on it more than the two-agent one (8.0\%). This is a limitation for sources without review titles.

Three lessons follow. First, an LLM asked to choose or revise the star did worse than the stack every time, which agrees with reports that LLMs struggle to self-correct without external feedback \cite{huang2024cannot}; this is why the LLM explains and checks instead. Second, extra views of the same words add noise, while a genuinely different view (the frozen LLM's hidden state, a fine-tuned encoder) adds signal. Third, an earlier version of this project used a four-agent design with a product-image verifier, a retrieval agent and a dissonance-based critic. An audit showed that the image verifier produced a constant output and the critic overrode the vote on only 0.4\% of reviews, so the design was replaced by the system described here.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{What the gain consists of.} The jump from 54.0\% to 67.5\% (eval) and from 53.4\% to 78.0\% (natural\_test) has three parts. Most of it comes from adding independent signals and a learned combiner: even the LoRA + Evidence stack reaches 65.5\% and 74.2\%. A smaller part comes from fitting the Reflector on more reviews (2\,000 instead of 500). The new agents (the probe and DeBERTa) add the rest, significantly on natural\_test but not on eval. The natural-test gain is partly a class-mix effect: Phase~1 was trained on balanced data and under-predicts 5-star reviews, which make up 65\% of natural\_test; this is why we report macro-F1 and the balanced test set beside it.

\textbf{Why ``agentic''.} The decision core alone is an ensemble. The system becomes agentic through the components that decide what to do next for each review: whether to answer or hand off, whether an explanation is good enough or must be rewritten, and (with two agents) whether the slow agent is needed. Each of these decisions is measured on the same frozen test sets, with fixed and broken counts, rather than assumed to help.

\textbf{Practical use.} For a retailer, the most useful output is probably the combination of the Hand-off agent and the checked explanation: more than half of real reviews receive an automatic star that is right 93\% of the time, with an explanation that quotes the review, and the rest receive a two-star range or go to a person.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) All main results use one dataset and one product category. (ii) The three-agent system depends on the review title (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). (iii) Part of the gain over the earliest stack comes from the larger Reflector fit set. (iv) The LLM judge in the Critic is the same model as the Explainer, and about 9\% of explanations still fail a check. (v) Sarcasm was too rare in the test sets to measure. (vi) The 92.6\% of the reference paper and our numbers come from different protocols and are not directly comparable. (vii) Five candidate agent sets were compared on dev2 before the final choice, by a rule fixed beforehand.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We reproduced a fine-tuned LLaMA-3 review-rating method under leakage control and explored it, finding a one-shot example leak, a cleaning step that removes most negative reviews, and template justifications. Building on these findings, a system of three independent agents and a learned Reflector improves exact star accuracy from 54.0\% to 67.5\% on balanced reviews and from 53.4\% to 78.0\% on the natural mix. An agentic layer makes the system's output checkable: a Critic loop raises faithful explanations from 62--64\% to 90.5--91.6\% without breaking any, the cited evidence passes a deletion test, and a Hand-off agent answers automatically only when confident. Three attempts to let an LLM decide the star failed, which suggests a division of labour: trained models decide, LLMs explain, and deterministic checks keep the explanations honest. Future work includes other product categories and datasets, an independent explanation judge, and agents that do not depend on the review title.
